## 1. Selective classification / failure detection: recommended evaluation practice and pitfalls

### Takeaway
Jaeger et al. identify that rank-only AUROC is blind to the effect of the scoring function on the underlying predictor, that AP is distorted by class imbalance, and that calibration is a separate task; they propose AURC as the primary metric. For a FIXED predictor (our case: the localization pipeline is not altered by the score) the "score changes the labels" pitfall does not arise, so AUROC-for-success is legitimate, but report AURC/risk-coverage with an oracle and a trivial baseline next to it, and quote AUROC with a cluster-bootstrap CI.

### Cited Findings
- Jaeger et al. (ICLR 2023 oral): failure-detection sub-fields (uncertainty, learned scores, OOD) use separate protocols that "either exclude a substantial part of relevant methods or ignore large parts of relevant failure sources"; they derive requirements for holistic evaluation and find a simple softmax-response baseline best overall — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15259)
- Pitfall 1: AUROC_f "exclusively measures discrimination of a classifier's success versus failure cases"; since a confidence function that changes the classifier also changes the failure labels, a method that lowers accuracy can look better by adding easy-to-detect failures — [Jaeger et al. HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.15259v2)
- Pitfall 2: datasets are biased towards many correct and few incorrect predictions, which inflates average-precision-style scores (AP_f); AP_f,err gives the highest scores at the beginning of training — [Jaeger et al. HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.15259v2)
- Pitfall 3: calibration is its own task; proper scoring rules (NLL, Brier) mix ranking and calibration and dilute focus — [Jaeger et al. HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.15259v2)
- Pitfall 4: OOD detection often evaluates distribution-shift detection, a surrogate, instead of the classifier's actual failures — [Jaeger et al. HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.15259v2)
- Recommendation: use AURC (area under risk-coverage curve, range [0,1], lower is better, combines accuracy and ranking) as primary metric; a single operating point may suit some use cases — [Jaeger et al. HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.15259v2)
- Jaeger et al. report AURC "averaged over 5 runs" (10 on one high-volatility dataset), with no formal confidence intervals in the visible text — [Jaeger et al. HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.15259v2)
- Zhu, Cheng, Zhang, Liu, "Rethinking Confidence Calibration for Failure Prediction", ECCV 2022: popular calibration methods often do NOT help failure prediction, attributed to worse separation of confidence between correct and incorrect samples; proposed fix is flat-minima training — [mlanthology](https://mlanthology.org/eccv/2022/zhu2022eccv-rethinking), [arXiv 2303.02970 (ar5iv)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.02970). (Seen as search summary only; the "OpenMix" paper named in the brief was NOT found/checked: UNVERIFIED.)
- Error estimates from cross-validation at neuroimaging-like sample sizes carry large error bars (about +-10 % accuracy in the author's experiments); standard error across folds underestimates it; changing the CV scheme does not fix it — [Varoquaux 2017, arXiv 1706.07581](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.07581) (summary only)

### Inferences
- Because the localizer is fixed and the reliability score is only a side output, the Jaeger "labels change with the score" pitfall does not apply. The relevant pitfalls are: (a) AUROC ignores base rate, so also show the risk-coverage curve whose floor is the overall failure rate; (b) with about 80 positives, AP/AUPR is noisy and prevalence-dependent, so report AUROC primary and AUPR only against the prevalence line (about 0.16); (c) the "selection of easy cases" artifact (central tumours) must be tested with the trivial "predict-centre" predictor evaluated on the SAME accepted subset (already in the repo notes).
- Recommended minimal report: AUROC (success vs score) with phantom-cluster bootstrap CI; AURC and the risk-coverage curve with the random line (flat at the overall risk), the oracle curve, and the trivial-predictor curve; selective risk at 2-3 pre-declared coverages (e.g. 50 %, 30 %, 20 %) with CI; all computed on out-of-fold scores.
- With the per-coverage numbers, remember the denominator: at 20 % coverage, n = 100 scans, about 5-15 phantoms effectively; CIs will be wide. Quote effective cluster counts.
- Ties: scores with many ties (a feature that is exactly 0 for 90 % of scans, as in the repo's interleave term) make AURC depend on tie handling. Break ties at random and average over several tie-breaks, or use the tie-corrected AUROC (Mann-Whitney with 0.5 credit). UNVERIFIED: the Jaeger appendix on this was truncated.

## 2. Uncertainty for localization / regression outputs: conformal, ensembles, TTA, and a ground-truth-free normalizing score

### Takeaway
Split conformal gives finite-sample MARGINAL coverage for any point predictor if calibration and test points are exchangeable; adaptivity (locally weighted / normalized scores, CQR) makes intervals vary with difficulty but only marginal coverage is guaranteed, and exact conditional coverage is impossible distribution-free. With phantom-grouped data exchangeability holds only across phantoms, so calibrate on whole phantoms and treat the effective sample as the number of phantoms. A ground-truth-free difficulty score (like a reliability score) can serve as the normalizer, but then it is a way to SIZE regions, not evidence that the score is predictive.

### Cited Findings
- Angelopoulos & Bates gentle introduction: wrap any model to get sets with user-chosen coverage, non-asymptotic, distribution-free; also covers risk control — [arXiv 2107.07511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511) (earlier note)
- Lei, G'Sell, Rinaldo, Tibshirani, Wasserman, JASA 113(523):1094-1111, 2018: prediction bands from any regression estimator with finite-sample marginal coverage; full vs split conformal vs jackknife trade accuracy against computation; Section 5.2 "Locally-Weighted Conformal Inference" produces bands with locally varying length, adapting to heteroscedasticity — [arXiv 1604.04173](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.04173) (search summary; Section 5.2 body not retrieved)
- Romano, Patterson, Candes, "Conformalized Quantile Regression" (NeurIPS 2019): combines conformal prediction with quantile regression, theoretical guarantee of valid coverage, "fully adaptive to heteroscedasticity", avoids constant-length intervals — [arXiv 1905.03222](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03222) (abstract)
- Barber, Candes, Ramdas, Tibshirani, "Predictive inference with the jackknife+": rigorous coverage "regardless of the distribution" under exchangeable training samples and any algorithm that treats training points symmetrically; the plain jackknife can have coverage that "may actually vanish"; near-exact coverage and similar length to jackknife require some stability — [arXiv 1905.02928](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.02928) (abstract; the 1-2 alpha level is stated in the paper, not on the fetched page, so UNVERIFIED here)
- Barber, Candes, Ramdas, Tibshirani, "The limits of distribution-free conditional predictive inference", Information and Inference 2021: exact conditional coverage cannot be guaranteed distribution-free; only approximate conditional coverage at tolerance delta, achievable mainly via marginal guarantees at an adjusted level — [arXiv 1903.04684](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.04684v2)
- Angelopoulos, Bates, Fisch, Lei, Schuster, "Conformal Risk Control": controls the EXPECTED value of any monotone loss, tight up to O(1/n); examples include false negative rate and graph distance; mentions extensions to distribution shift — [arXiv 2208.02814](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814) (abstract)
- Bates, Angelopoulos, Lei, Malik, Jordan, "Distribution-free, risk-controlling prediction sets" / Angelopoulos et al. "Learn then Test": risk control cast as multiple-hypothesis testing with finite-sample guarantees, without refitting, examples include IoU control in segmentation — [arXiv 2110.01052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01052). The fetched arXiv id 2110.01052 is the Learn-then-Test paper; the RCPS paper (Bates et al., JACM 2021) was not separately fetched: UNVERIFIED.
- Conformal localization: Yang & Pavone, "Object Pose Estimation with Statistical Guarantees: Conformal Keypoint Detection and Geometric Uncertainty Propagation", CVPR 2023: inductive conformal prediction turns heatmap keypoint detections into circular/elliptical prediction sets with marginal coverage (e.g. 90 %); nonconformity takes the max across keypoints; the authors note set size depends on the nonconformity function and can be conservative — [arXiv 2303.12246](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.12246); a NeurIPS 2022 workshop version reports sets grow for hard keypoints — [NeurIPS virtual page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2022/60350)
- Jonkers et al., multi-output conformal prediction for anatomical landmarks in 2D/3D, non-convex prediction regions (M-R2CCP, M-R2C2R) — [arXiv 2503.14106](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14106v1) (summary only)
- Clustered data violates exchangeability; options are hierarchical/symmetry-based conformal (mostly marginal guarantees), weighted non-exchangeable conformal (Barber, Candes, Ramdas, Tibshirani, Ann. Stat. 2023, "Conformal prediction beyond exchangeability"), or group-conditional (Mondrian-style) calibration, which needs enough calibration data per group; hierarchical CP can be conservative when the number of calibration groups is small — [Beyond exchangeability](https://arxiv.org/html/2202.13415v5), [cluster-trial CP, arXiv 2401.01977](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.01977), [Generalized Hierarchical CP, arXiv 2608.15500](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15500). These 2026 items were seen only through a search summary: UNVERIFIED.
- Test-time augmentation (Wang et al., Neurocomputing 2019): treat augmented inputs as samples of an acquisition model; variance/entropy over the augmented predictions estimates aleatoric uncertainty, better than test-time dropout alone and reduces overconfident wrong predictions (fetal brain, brain-tumour MRI segmentation) — [arXiv 1807.07356](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.07356), [PMC6783308](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6783308/)

### Inferences
- Failure here is binary (error > tolerance), so the natural conformal object is not an interval but a risk statement. Two usable designs: (1) Conformal risk control with loss L = 1{error > tol} restricted to accepted scans is NOT monotone in the way the theorem needs unless the acceptance rule is a threshold lambda on the score with loss defined over scans (loss = 1{error > tol and score >= lambda}); that loss is monotone non-increasing in lambda, so CRC applies in principle and yields a lambda with expected "accepted-failure rate" <= alpha. This is my reading of the abstract, not a published example: UNVERIFIED. (2) Learn-then-Test / Hoeffding-Bentkus style selective-risk bound at a fixed lambda (Geifman & El-Yaniv's bound in the earlier note is the same idea).
- With about 80 positives and only about 20-50 failures-vs-successes per tail, a guaranteed risk level will be very loose (O(1/sqrt(effective n))); report the bound honestly or do not claim a guarantee. Grouped calibration reduces effective n further towards the number of phantoms.
- A ground-truth-free difficulty score s(x) can enter as a normalizer: nonconformity = |error| / s(x) (locally weighted conformal, Lei et al. Sect. 5.2) giving a region radius r(x) = q_hat * s(x). Coverage is marginal only; whether s(x) actually tracks error shows up as the spread of r(x) vs realized error (e.g. by-score-decile empirical coverage). If s is uninformative, regions become near-constant radius, which is the correct degenerate behaviour. Useful sanity check, not a substitute for the AUROC test.
- Localization-specific: a point error is 2-D, so use a radial nonconformity (Euclidean error) rather than per-axis intervals; the conformal quantile of radial error gives a circle (as in Yang & Pavone). Then "success" at tolerance tol is exactly whether the circle radius needed is below tol, linking the conformal radius to the abstention rule: abstain when r(x) > tol.
- Ground-truth-free consistency scores are the localization analogue of ensemble / TTA disagreement: spread of argmax positions across K sub-apertures, sub-bands, bootstrap resamples of channels, or small geometric perturbations (radius, centre) of the forward model. Disagreement estimates variance only; shared bias (central pull) is invisible, consistent with the earlier note and with the TTA paper covering aleatoric spread, not systematic error. Jackknife+/CV+ style aggregation is a possible way to build the spread without refitting but is designed for regression-function fits with a training set, which does not exist here for a non-learned pipeline; there is no training stage to leave out, so plain delete-one-subset-of-channels spread is the analogue (my analogy, UNVERIFIED).
- Under grouped data: use a calibration set made of whole phantoms; keep the number of calibration phantoms as large as possible (at least 30 for a 90 % statement is my rule of thumb, not a sourced one); alternatively use leave-one-phantom-out "cross-conformal" quantiles (jackknife+ style, Vovk's cross-conformal), which gives up the clean guarantee but uses all data.

## 3. Combining a few weak features: rank average vs penalized logistic vs boosting; sample size

### Takeaway
With about 80 events, penalized (ridge / L2 or a very small number of pre-specified, sign-constrained terms) logistic regression or a plain rank/z-score average are the defensible options; gradient boosting is not supportable. The sample-size literature (Riley et al.) replaces events-per-variable with criteria on expected shrinkage and overfitting, but supports the principle that the number of candidate predictor parameters must be small relative to the events.

### Cited Findings
- Riley et al., Statistics in Medicine 2019 (parts I and II): the old rule of 10 events per candidate predictor parameter (EPP) is superseded by performance-based sample-size criteria (expected shrinkage, overfitting limits, anticipated Cox-Snell R^2); a rare outcome with a few strong predictors may need fewer than 10 EPP, many uncertain predictors need more; implemented in R `pmsampsize` — [BMC review search result](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12874-023-02008-1), [Pate, Riley et al. arXiv 2207.12892](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2207.12892) (secondary summaries; the original Riley papers were not opened)
- A 2023 systematic review found sample-size requirements are often not considered in binary-outcome prediction model studies — [BMC Med Res Methodol 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s12874-023-02008-1)
- Platt-style (parametric, one or two parameters) recalibration is preferred over isotonic when data are scarce, because isotonic overfits — [Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana, ICML 2005](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2005/niculescumizil2005icml-predicting); temperature scaling (single-parameter Platt) often most effective in deep nets — [Guo et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1706.04599)
- Standard CV confidence intervals can have far-below-nominal coverage because fold errors are correlated; in a sparse logistic example intervals needed to be about 1.6x wider; nested CV gives better variance estimates; K-fold on small samples can be strongly biased relative to nested CV or a train/test split — [Bates, Hastie, Tibshirani, JASA 2023 (arXiv 2104.00673)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.00673v3); [PMC6837442 (search result, not opened)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6837442)

### Inferences
- With n_events = 80 and a shrinkage target of 0.9, Riley-style bounds typically allow only a handful of candidate parameters for a weak predictor (anticipated Cox-Snell R^2 for AUROC about 0.6 is around 0.02-0.03, which is very small, implying that required n is far larger than 500). I did not compute this with `pmsampsize`; do it, because it likely shows the data cannot support fitting more than 2-3 weights. UNVERIFIED numeric claim, explicitly a to-do.
- Ranking the options for this regime:
  1. Pre-specified fixed-direction rank (or z-score) average of at most 3-4 features, direction fixed from physics BEFORE looking at outcomes: zero fitted parameters, so no optimism; cost is no weighting. Prefer this as the primary reported score.
  2. L2-penalized (ridge) logistic regression on standardized features with sign constraints (non-negative weights after orienting features) and penalty chosen by inner grouped CV; one or two effective parameters. Report coefficient stability across folds. Sign constraints can be obtained with a projected fit or by flipping features to the pre-specified orientation and clipping at 0 (my suggestion).
  3. Gradient boosting / random forest: with about 80 events and weak features, expect optimism and unstable ranking; monotone constraints reduce variance but do not remove the need for tuning; not recommended as primary. If tried, treat as an exploratory comparator under the same nested grouped CV, with strong shrinkage, depth <= 2, monotone constraints, and report that it is exploratory.
- Nested grouped CV protocol: outer loop = leave-one-phantom-out or repeated GroupKFold (e.g. 5 folds x 20 repeats with different random phantom partitions) giving out-of-fold scores; inner loop = grouped CV on the training phantoms for penalty and any feature selection; EVERY data-driven step (standardization parameters, orientation choice, feature selection, threshold) lives inside the loop. Report the distribution of the AUROC over repeats and a cluster-bootstrap CI that resamples phantoms of the pooled out-of-fold scores; note (Bates et al.) that this CI is approximate and likely still a bit narrow; widening or using nested-CV variance estimates is more conservative.
- Pooled AUROC over out-of-fold scores mixes folds whose score scale differs. Safer: convert each fold's out-of-fold score to a within-fold rank, or report the mean of per-fold AUROCs together with the pooled value.
- Declaring the composite structure and weights as pre-specified before seeing outcomes avoids the multiplicity burden; each additional candidate composite (rank mean, ridge, boosted) adds to the correction family (Holm, in earlier note).

## 4. Avoiding leakage with grouped scans; confounder (object size) adjustment of AUROC; cluster-aware inference

### Takeaway
Group all splits by phantom and resample phantoms for inference. Obuchowski's clustered AUC and the phantom cluster bootstrap are the right inference tools; for a confounder such as size, report stratified AUROC and the covariate-adjusted ROC (Janes & Pepe) or a size-matched comparison, because pooled AUROC can reflect size differences rather than within-size discrimination.

### Cited Findings
- Roberts et al., Ecography 40(8):913-929, 2017: random CV ignores dependence and seriously underestimates predictive error; blocked CV recommended wherever dependence structure exists, even when residuals show no obvious correlation; blocking can induce extrapolation and overestimate interpolation error, so choose blocking to match the intended prediction target — [Regensburg record](https://pred.uni-regensburg.de/id/eprint/1455/), [PDF mirror](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/roberts-et-al-2017-ecography.pdf)
- Obuchowski, "Nonparametric Analysis of Clustered ROC Curve Data", Biometrics 1997: extends DeLong's structural-components method to clustered data using design effect / effective sample size from Rao & Scott; needs no specification of the intra-cluster correlation structure; R functions at Cleveland Clinic Lerner — [clusteredROC help](https://www.lerner.ccf.org/quantitative-health/documents/clusteredROC_help.pdf), [summary page](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/nonparametric-analysis-of-clustered-roc-curve-data/812073859254583296-922)
- Wu (2020): Obuchowski's estimator weights all within- and between-cluster pairs equally, which is less efficient; a weighted alternative gains substantially at moderate intra-cluster correlation with large clusters — [IDEAS record, Comm. Stat. 49(6):1446-1461](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/lstaxx/v49y2020i6p1446-1461.html). A paper on "estimation problems in small sample sizes" for clustered diagnostic data exists — [EconBiz record](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/nonparametric-analysis-of-clustered-data-in-diagnostic-trials-estimation-problems-in-small-sample-sizes/10005165397) (title only seen).
- Janes & Pepe, Biometrika 96(2):371-382, 2009: the covariate-adjusted ROC (AROC) curve is the ROC obtained with covariate-specific thresholds such that the false-positive fraction is fixed within each covariate stratum; if the covariate only shifts marker values it equals the common covariate-specific ROC; otherwise it is a weighted average of stratum TPFs; pooled ROC can look too optimistic when case proportions differ across strata (e.g. centres) — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:biomet:v:96:y:2009:i:2:p:371-382), [NCI/EDRN note](https://edrn.cancer.gov/data-and-resources/publications/20046933-1473-accommodating-covariates-in-roc-analysis); Janes, Longton, Pepe, Stata Journal 9(1):17-39, 2009, three ways to use covariates in ROC analysis — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tsj:stataj:v:9:y:2009:i:1:p:17-39)
- Cluster bootstrap consistency: Field & Welsh 2007 (earlier note).

### Inferences
- Splits: every train/test or fold split by phantom, never by scan; this includes any threshold choice, standardization, feature-direction choice, and ANY tuning of the pipeline hyperparameters that produced the scores (the localizer's own parameters were chosen on this dataset in the original report; that is a form of leakage that grouped CV on the reliability score cannot fix; state it as a limitation).
- Leave-one-group-out with 66 groups is nearly unbiased and deterministic but has high variance per fold (about 1.5 phantoms) and cannot give per-fold AUROC; repeated 5- or 6-fold GroupKFold is the practical alternative (Varoquaux: scheme matters less than sample size).
- Inference on AUROC: (a) cluster bootstrap over phantoms (resample phantoms with replacement, take all their scans, recompute AUROC; report percentile or BCa interval; redo selection steps inside); (b) Obuchowski clustered DeLong as an analytic cross-check, noting its small-sample caveat (Wu, and the EconBiz small-sample item); (c) plain DeLong or Hanley-McNeil treats scans as independent and will be too narrow; they are not used for the headline CI. With about 66 phantoms of which maybe 30-40 contain at least one success, resampling is noisy: check that the bootstrap distribution is not degenerate (resamples with no positives) and state the number of phantoms with at least one success and one failure.
- Permutation test for AUROC > 0.5: permute the SUCCESS labels at the PHANTOM level (permute phantom-level label vectors across phantoms of equal size) or permute scores across phantoms, not individual scans, so the null preserves within-phantom correlation. Common sense design, UNVERIFIED as a cited method.
- Object-size confounding: if the score correlates with size (e.g. distance from centre, tumour size, shell radius) and so does success, pooled AUROC may merely reflect size. Tools: (1) stratified AUROC within bins of the confounder (3-4 bins), then a pooled estimate, e.g. weighting by stratum pair counts or a Mantel-Haenszel-style combination; (2) AROC (Janes & Pepe): rank each scan's score against the failure-group scores in its own stratum, then compute the ROC of those placement values; (3) adjusting via logistic regression with the confounder as a covariate and reporting the conditional (adjusted) AUROC / the score's coefficient with a cluster-robust SE; (4) matching successes with failures of similar size. (2) is the cleanest conceptually; with 80 positives use 3 strata, not more. Adding "partial AUROC" restricted to a low-false-positive-rate region is relevant only when the operating regime is high precision (accept few scans); I found no source tying it to this case, standard definition only (UNVERIFIED).
- Report the confounder-only baseline: AUROC of the confounder alone (and of the trivial centre prediction's error) predicting success. If score AUROC adjusted for size is not above the confounder-only AUROC, the score adds nothing beyond size.

## 5. Calibration, decision-curve and cost-aware reporting for abstention

### Takeaway
Calibration is secondary here (ranking is what selects scans), and at 80 events calibration curves are noisy; if a probability is reported, use a one- or two-parameter logistic recalibration and report calibration intercept and slope with CIs instead of ECE. For decisions, decision-curve / net-benefit and an explicit cost ratio between a wrong accepted localization and a rejected correct one give a value-based view that complements the risk-coverage curve.

### Cited Findings
- Van Calster et al., "Calibration: the Achilles heel of predictive analytics", BMC Medicine 17:230, 2019: levels mean/weak/moderate/strong calibration; weak = no systematic over/under-estimation and not too extreme or too modest predictions; calibration curves at validation need sufficiently large samples; overfitting is a common cause of poor calibration — [PMC6912996](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6912996), [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/calibration-the-achilles-heel-of-predictive-analytics)
- ECE is binning-dependent: bins and details leave ambiguity; later papers argue ECE has flaws and is not testable in many practical cases — [Measuring calibration in deep learning, arXiv 1904.01685](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.01685), [arXiv 2502.19851](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.19851v2) (search summaries)
- Jaeger et al.: calibration is its own task; NLL/Brier mix ranking and calibration — [Jaeger HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2211.15259v2)
- Vickers & Elkin, "Decision curve analysis", Med Decis Making 26(6):565-574, 2006: net benefit = TP/n - FP/n * pt/(1-pt) across threshold probabilities pt, with "treat all" (crossing the y-axis at prevalence) and "treat none" (zero) references; needs no external cost data; applies to continuous or binary outputs — [PMC2577036](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2577036), [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:sae:medema:v:26:y:2006:i:6:p:565-574). A 2023 paper argues null-hypothesis testing of decision curves is of questionable value and suggests value-of-information or probability of benefit — [Vickers et al. 2023 via search result](https://synapse.mskcc.org/synapse/works/216480)
- Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005 and Guo et al. 2017 on Platt / temperature scaling over isotonic when data are small (see section 3).

### Inferences
- Abstention net benefit: define "accept" = report the localization, with benefit for an accepted correct localization and cost c for an accepted incorrect one; the net benefit curve is NB(pt) = TP/n - FP/n * pt/(1-pt), where "positive" = accept and TP = accepted successes. Since the score is not a probability, first map it to an out-of-fold probability with a 1-2 parameter logistic recalibration (cross-fitted by phantom), then plot NB against pt with "accept all" and "accept none" lines. The curve is only meaningful where the model beats accept-all; with prevalence of about 16 %, accept-all has small positive NB only at low pt, so the informative region is narrow. Application of DCA to abstention rather than treatment decisions is my transfer of the method, not something Vickers & Elkin discuss: UNVERIFIED.
- Cost-aware alternative without a probability: pick the cost ratio r = cost(accepted failure) / benefit(accepted success) and plot expected utility = (successes accepted) - r * (failures accepted) as a function of coverage; report the coverage maximizing utility only for a range of r, with CI via phantom cluster bootstrap. This avoids a probability model altogether.
- Calibration reporting at this size: calibration intercept and slope (logistic recalibration of out-of-fold log-odds) with cluster-bootstrap CI; a reliability plot with at most 4-5 equal-count bins and CIs; do not report ECE as a headline. Slope substantially below 1 means overfitted composites. In the regime of weak features, the expected slope is below 1 and perfect calibration of the raw score is not a goal.
